Choose the subject and shot size

You can write two framing prompts for the same product.

When the frame tries to show everything, the subject loses impact. Shot size determines what the audience can make out.

In 1 minute

  1. State the main element and the action.
  2. A wide shot shows the setting.
  3. A close-up shows one specific part.

1Subject and action: what needs to be there

The subject is the image’s main element; the action is what happens to it.

In a still scene, the relationship to the surface also matters: a mug resting on a table is different from a mug suspended in the air.

Quantity and position reduce ambiguity.

A prompt like “beautiful product” does not specify shape, material, or situation.

The description should prioritize recognizable characteristics that serve the goal.

Joana chooses the mug as the subject. She removes objects that compete with the ceramic piece from the request.

2Framing and viewpoint

Framing is the part of the scene that appears in the image.

A wide shot shows context; a medium shot balances the object and setting; a close-up shows one area.

Viewpoint describes the camera’s position: above, at the object’s height, or below.

The same mug can communicate use, shape, or finish depending on the crop.

The size of the subject in the image changes what a learner and audience can inspect.

Mauro prepares two pieces: one shows the coffee table; the other shows the handle’s finish.

3Check the decision against its use

The shot should match the buyer’s question. A scene-setting image cannot prove a small detail about the finish.

Describe the light and surface

You can distinguish a lighting decision from a material decision.

A surface can look like plastic when the intention was ceramic. Light and texture need to work together.

In 1 minute

  1. Name the light source.
  2. Say whether the shadow is soft or defined.
  3. Describe the material with visible cues.

1Light: source, direction, and quality

The source is where the light comes from, such as a window.

Direction is its position relative to the object.

Quality describes the shadow transition: hard, with a defined edge; or soft, with a gradual transition.

The light’s color is another decision; it does not replace the ones above.

Light makes volume and material legible.

Saying only “cinematic lighting” leaves the shadows, reflections, and separation from the background unspecified.

Joana wants to show the matte glaze. She avoids asking for intense reflections that hide the piece’s texture.

2Texture: how the surface responds

Texture is the pattern of small variations on a surface.

Material is what an object appears to be made of.

Matte ceramic, polished metal, and linen fabric call for different reflections and details; these cues help us interpret the image.

Adding “ultra-detailed” does not fix an incoherent material.

For Aurora, small glaze variations are useful; cracks and exaggerated roughness would change how the product is perceived.

Mauro places the mug near a window to photograph it. He notices the soft shadow and small variations in the matte surface.

3Check the decision against its use

Check that the requested finish remains. Ceramic can be glossy, but a piece described as matte should not turn into a mirror-like surface.

Separate format, identity, and style

You can choose the image’s aspect ratio and assign roles to two references.

A beautiful reference can change the product without solving the format. These are different decisions and need separate instructions.

In 1 minute

  1. Aspect ratio describes the frame’s shape.
  2. Identity is what makes the object recognizable as the same one.
  3. Style guides the scene’s appearance.

1Aspect ratio is not resolution

Aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height.

A square has a 1:1 aspect ratio.

Resolution is the image dimensions in dots, called pixels.

Files with 512 by 512 and 1024 by 1024 pixels are still square.

Choosing the aspect ratio first helps with composition.

Adding pixels later cannot restore a cropped handle or make room for a title.

Joana chooses a square format for the product sheet. An image with more pixels does not change its shape by itself.

2References for identity and style

Identity is the appearance that lets you recognize the same product.

Style is the scene’s language of color, light, and texture.

A photo of the mug guides its shape.

Another image can guide only the light without changing the object.

Separating these roles prevents the product’s appearance from changing when you ask for a new atmosphere.

Check the identity first, then the light and colors.

Mauro uses one photo for the mug and another for the setting. He checks that the handle remains recognizable in both versions.

3Check the decision against its use

Compare width and height separately from the pixel count. Then check the object before judging the atmosphere.

Mauro uses this note to decide: style → palette and light from photo B.
